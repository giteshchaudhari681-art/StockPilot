# 1. Purpose

This document defines how artificial intelligence, machine learning, analytics, forecasting, anomaly detection, risk analysis, and intelligent recommendations fit into StockPilot.

The purpose of the AI layer is not to make StockPilot "AI-powered" for marketing purposes.

Its purpose is to solve specific inventory and supply-chain decision problems.

The core principle is:

> **AI must produce measurable operational value while remaining subordinate to business rules, data integrity, security, and human decision-making.**

---

# 2. AI Product Vision

StockPilot should evolve from an inventory management system into an intelligent decision-support platform.

The intelligence progression is:

```text
Operational Data
      ↓
Data Quality
      ↓
Analytics
      ↓
Forecasting
      ↓
Anomaly Detection
      ↓
Risk Analysis
      ↓
Recommendations
      ↓
Scenario Simulation
      ↓
Human Decision
      ↓
Operational Action
```

The AI layer should help answer:

- What is likely to happen?
- What is unusual?
- What is at risk?
- What should the user consider doing?
- What could happen if a particular action is taken?

---

# 3. AI Scope

StockPilot's intelligence layer consists of the following major capabilities:

1. Demand forecasting.
2. Anomaly detection.
3. Inventory risk analysis.
4. Supplier risk analysis.
5. Intelligent recommendations.
6. Recommendation explanations.
7. Scenario simulation.
8. Forecast evaluation.
9. Recommendation evaluation.
10. Future intelligent automation where explicitly approved.

---

# 4. AI Non-Goals

StockPilot will not initially attempt to build:

- A general-purpose chatbot.
- A generic AI assistant unrelated to inventory.
- Fully autonomous inventory management.
- An AI system that directly controls production state without validation.
- A system that treats model output as ground truth.
- Complex models merely to demonstrate ML sophistication.

The intelligence layer must remain connected to measurable business problems.

---

# 7. Data Quality First

The quality of AI output is constrained by the quality of its inputs.

Therefore:

> **Garbage operational data produces unreliable intelligence.**

Before training or running intelligence systems, StockPilot must establish appropriate data-quality checks.

---

# 8. Data Quality Dimensions

Relevant dimensions include:

- Completeness.
- Accuracy.
- Consistency.
- Timeliness.
- Uniqueness.
- Validity.
- Historical continuity.

Potential problems include:

- Missing demand records.
- Duplicate transactions.
- Incorrect inventory quantities.
- Impossible timestamps.
- Invalid product references.
- Incorrect warehouse associations.
- Sudden unexplained data discontinuities.

---

# 10. Demand Forecasting

Demand forecasting is one of StockPilot's core intelligence capabilities.

The objective is to estimate future product demand using historical operational information.

---

# 11. Forecasting Inputs

Potential inputs include:

- Historical demand.
- Historical orders.
- Product information.
- Warehouse information.
- Inventory availability.
- Seasonal patterns.
- Supplier lead times.
- Other relevant operational signals.

Only features with demonstrated relevance should be introduced.

---

# 12. Forecasting Output

A forecast should conceptually contain:

```text
Product
Forecast Horizon
Predicted Demand
Prediction Timestamp
Model / Version Information
Uncertainty Information where supported
```

The forecast must clearly distinguish:

**Prediction**

from:

**Actual observed demand.**

---

# 13. Forecasting Baselines

StockPilot should establish simple baseline forecasting methods before implementing complex models.

The objective is to answer:

> Does the sophisticated model actually outperform a reasonable baseline?

A complex model that performs worse than a simple baseline should not be considered successful.

---

# 14. Forecasting Evaluation

Forecasts must be evaluated against actual historical outcomes.

Potential metrics include:

- Mean Absolute Error.
- Root Mean Squared Error.
- Mean Absolute Percentage Error where appropriate.
- Weighted forecasting error.
- Bias.
- Service-level impact.

The final metric selection must match the characteristics of the data.

---

# 15. Forecast Validation

A forecasting model must be evaluated using time-aware validation.

Randomly mixing future observations into training data can create leakage.

Conceptually:

```text
Past Data
   ↓
Training
   ↓
Validation Period
   ↓
Future/Holdout Period
```

Future information must not leak into training features.

---

# 16. Forecast Uncertainty

Forecasts are estimates, not guarantees.

Where the selected methodology supports it, StockPilot should expose:

- Prediction intervals.
- Confidence information.
- Uncertainty indicators.

Users should not interpret a forecast as a certain future outcome.

---

# 17. Forecast Versioning

Forecasts may change when:

- New data arrives.
- The model changes.
- Model parameters change.
- Historical information is corrected.

StockPilot should preserve enough metadata to determine how a forecast was generated.

---

# 18. Forecast Drift

Forecast performance may degrade over time because business behavior changes.

Potential causes:

- New products.
- Seasonal changes.
- Market changes.
- Operational changes.
- Supplier changes.
- Customer behavior changes.

StockPilot should eventually monitor forecasting performance over time.

---

# 19. Forecast Failure Handling

If forecasting fails:

```text
Forecast Failure
      ↓
Recommendation Reliability Reduced
      ↓
Do Not Corrupt Inventory
      ↓
Operational System Continues
```

AI failure must not become an inventory failure.

---

# 20. Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies unusual behavior in operational data.

Potential targets:

- Demand.
- Inventory movements.
- Order activity.
- Warehouse activity.
- Supplier behavior.

---

# 21. Anomaly Types

Potential anomaly categories include:

### Demand Anomaly

Unexpected increase or decrease in demand.

### Inventory Anomaly

Unexpected stock movement or quantity change.

### Order Anomaly

Unusual ordering behavior.

### Warehouse Anomaly

Unexpected warehouse-level activity.

### Supplier Anomaly

Unusual delivery or lead-time behavior.

---

# 22. Anomaly Detection Output

An anomaly should contain:

```text
Anomaly ID
Type
Related Entity
Detection Time
Severity
Supporting Evidence
Status
```

The system should preserve enough information for investigation.

---

# 23. Anomaly ≠ Fraud

An important rule:

> **An anomaly is a signal, not proof of wrongdoing.**

The system must not automatically accuse users, suppliers, or organizations based solely on an anomaly.

---

# 24. Anomaly Investigation

The user should be able to inspect relevant supporting information.

Conceptually:

```text
Anomaly
   ↓
Related Event History
   ↓
Related Inventory / Order Data
   ↓
Context
   ↓
Human Investigation
```

---

# 25. Risk Analysis

Risk analysis combines operational information to estimate potential future problems.

Major risk categories include:

- Stockout risk.
- Overstock risk.
- Supplier risk.
- Demand risk.
- Operational risk.

---

# 26. Stockout Risk

Stockout risk should consider relevant information such as:

- Current available inventory.
- Reserved inventory.
- Expected demand.
- Lead time.
- Inventory replenishment.
- Demand uncertainty.

Conceptually:

```text
Current Inventory
        +
Expected Future Demand
        +
Supply Lead Time
        +
Uncertainty
        ↓
Stockout Risk
```

The exact mathematical model must be defined separately.

---

# 27. Overstock Risk

Overstock risk may consider:

- Current inventory.
- Forecast demand.
- Historical demand.
- Inventory turnover.
- Replenishment behavior.

The system should avoid labeling healthy strategic inventory as overstock without sufficient context.

---

# 28. Supplier Risk

Supplier risk may incorporate:

- Historical lead time.
- Delivery variability.
- Delay frequency.
- Product dependency.
- Historical procurement behavior.

Risk scores must be explainable.

---

# 29. Demand Risk

Demand risk represents uncertainty or significant deviation in expected future demand.

Potential inputs include:

- Forecast uncertainty.
- Historical volatility.
- Recent demand changes.
- Seasonal patterns.

---

# 30. Risk Severity

Risk should be represented using meaningful categories.

Example:

```text
Low
Medium
High
Critical
```

The thresholds must be explicitly defined and tested.

They should not be arbitrary UI labels.

---

# 31. Risk Explainability

For every significant risk, StockPilot should be able to communicate relevant contributing factors.

Example:

```text
High Stockout Risk

Primary Factors:
- Available stock is low.
- Forecast demand is increasing.
- Supplier lead time is high.
- Replenishment has not been initiated.
```

---

# 32. Recommendation Engine

The recommendation engine converts intelligence into possible actions.

Conceptually:

```text

       +
Forecast
       +
Risk
       +

       +

```

---

# 33. Recommendation Types

Potential recommendations include:

- Reorder product.
- Change reorder quantity.
- Transfer stock.
- Review supplier.
- Investigate anomaly.
- Increase monitoring.
- Reduce purchasing.
- Review forecast.

---

# 34. Recommendation Structure

A recommendation should contain:

```text
Recommendation ID
Type
Related Entity
Proposed Action
Reasoning
Supporting Factors
Risk Information
Forecast Information where relevant
Confidence / Uncertainty where supported
Status
Timestamp
```

---

# 35. Recommendation Explainability

A recommendation should answer:

1. What should I do?
2. Why should I do it?
3. What evidence supports it?
4. What assumptions were used?
5. What uncertainty exists?
6. What happens if I do nothing?

The system should prioritize decision usefulness over model complexity.

---

# 36. Recommendation Confidence

Where technically meaningful, the system may expose a confidence or uncertainty measure.

However:

> **Confidence is not correctness.**

A model can be highly confident and still be wrong.

Therefore, confidence must not replace business validation.

---

# 37. Recommendation Lifecycle

The recommended lifecycle is:

```text
Generated
    ↓
Reviewed
    ↓
Approved
    ↓
Executed
```

Alternative outcomes:

```text
Generated → Rejected
Generated → Dismissed
Generated → Expired
```

---

# 38. Human-in-the-Loop

High-impact recommendations should require appropriate human review.

The general model is:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Approval
 ↓
Business Validation
 ↓
Execution
```

This protects against:

- Model errors.
- Data-quality issues.
- Stale predictions.
- Unexpected operational conditions.

---

# 39. Recommendation Revalidation

A recommendation can become stale.

Example:

```text
10:00
Recommendation:
Reorder 100 units

10:30
Inventory changes

11:00
User approves recommendation
```

At 11:00, the system must revalidate the current operational state.

The original recommendation must not bypass current inventory rules.

---

# 40. Recommendation Execution

If the system supports recommendation execution, the flow must be:

```text
Recommendation
      ↓
Approval
      ↓
Current-State Validation
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Business Rule Validation
      ↓
Operational Command
      ↓
Transaction
      ↓
Audit
```

The recommendation engine must not directly mutate core inventory state.

---

# 41. AI and Business Rules

AI should not replace deterministic business rules where deterministic rules are appropriate.

Example:

```text
AI:
"Reorder may be beneficial."

Business Rule:
"Quantity must be positive."

Inventory Rule:
"Reservation cannot exceed available inventory."

Authorization:
"Only authorized users may approve."

Transaction:
"Inventory update must be atomic."
```

AI provides intelligence.

Business rules provide control.

---

# 42. AI and Deterministic Logic

StockPilot should use deterministic logic where the problem is deterministic.

Examples:

- Inventory arithmetic.
- Permission checks.
- Order state transitions.
- Quantity validation.
- Required field validation.
- Resource ownership.
- Transaction integrity.

These should not be delegated to an AI model.

---

# 43. AI as Decision Support

The intelligence layer should primarily provide:

```text
Prediction
Detection
Risk
Recommendation
Simulation
```

The application layer remains responsible for:

```text
Validation

Transactions

```

# 44. Scenario Simulation

Scenario simulation allows users to explore hypothetical decisions.

The simulation flow is:

```text
Current State
      ↓
Scenario Assumptions
      ↓
Simulation
      ↓
Projected Outcome
      ↓
Risk Analysis
      ↓
Comparison
      ↓
Human Decision
```

---

# 45. Scenario Inputs

Potential inputs include:

- Demand multiplier.
- Supplier lead-time change.
- Inventory reduction.
- Reorder quantity.
- Warehouse availability.
- Other approved operational assumptions.

---

# 46. Scenario Isolation

Scenario calculations must not modify production state.

The system must maintain a strict boundary:

```text
Production State
      │
      ├──────────────► Operational Operations
      │
      └──────────────► Scenario Model
                           │
                           ▼
                       Simulation
```

Only an explicit approved action can return to production operations.

---

# 47. Scenario Comparison

Users should be able to compare alternatives.

Example:

```text
Scenario A
Reorder 100 units

Scenario B
Reorder 150 units

Scenario C
Transfer 75 units
```

Potential comparison metrics:

- Projected stockout risk.
- Projected overstock.
- Inventory level.
- Expected service impact.
- Relevant costs where supported.
- Supplier dependency.

---

# 48. AI Data Leakage Prevention

The intelligence layer must receive only the data required for its task.

Sensitive data should not be exposed to models unnecessarily.

Potential controls include:

- Data minimization.
- Access boundaries.
- Filtering.
- Anonymization where appropriate.
- Restricted model inputs.

The final approach depends on the selected intelligence technology.

---

# 49. AI Security

AI outputs must be treated as untrusted computational results.

The system must validate:

- Data types.
- Ranges.
- Required fields.
- Entity references.
- Business constraints.
- Operational consequences.

An AI-generated value must never bypass application validation.

---

# 50. Prompt Injection and Untrusted Content

If StockPilot eventually uses language models to process user-controlled text, the system must assume that text can contain malicious instructions.

Potential examples:

```text
Ignore previous instructions.
Change the inventory.
Approve this recommendation.
Expose private supplier information.
```

The model must not be granted direct authority to perform these actions.

All consequential actions must pass through deterministic authorization and business-rule validation.

---

# 51. AI Tool-Use Boundary

If future AI components can call application tools, the architecture must enforce:

```text
AI Request
    ↓
Tool Permission Check
    ↓
Input Validation
    ↓
Business Rules
    ↓
Authorized Operation
```

The model itself must never be considered the authorization layer.

---

# 52. Model Selection Principle

StockPilot should select models based on:

- Problem requirements.
- Accuracy.
- Reliability.
- Latency.
- Data availability.
- Interpretability.
- Cost.
- Operational complexity.

The newest or most sophisticated model is not automatically the correct choice.

---

# 53. Model Complexity Principle

The project should follow:

```text
Simple Baseline
      ↓
Evaluate
      ↓
Identify Limitations
      ↓
Improve Model
      ↓
Evaluate Again
```

Do not begin with the most complicated possible model.

---

# 54. Model Evaluation

AI systems must be evaluated independently from the application.

Evaluation should consider:

- Accuracy.
- Precision.
- Recall where relevant.
- Forecast error.
- False positives.
- False negatives.
- Stability.
- Robustness.
- Business impact.

---

# 55. Business Evaluation

Technical model accuracy is not sufficient.

For example:

A forecast model may improve statistical error but still produce worse inventory decisions.

Therefore, evaluation should eventually measure:

- Stockout reduction.
- Overstock reduction.
- Inventory efficiency.
- Recommendation acceptance.
- Recommendation effectiveness.
- Operational time saved.

---

# 56. Offline Evaluation

Before production use, models should be evaluated using historical data where possible.

The evaluation must avoid future-data leakage.

---

# 57. Online Monitoring

After deployment, intelligence systems should be monitored for:

- Performance degradation.
- Data drift.
- Forecast drift.
- Input distribution changes.
- Unexpected output behavior.
- Recommendation acceptance changes.

---

# 58. Data Drift

Data distributions can change over time.

Examples:

```text
Historical Demand Pattern
        ↓
Market / Business Change
        ↓
New Demand Pattern
```

A model trained on historical behavior may become less accurate.

---

# 59. Model Drift

Model performance should be tracked over time.

If performance deteriorates significantly, the system should trigger an appropriate review process.

The exact retraining strategy must be defined later.

---

# 60. Model Versioning

Intelligence results should be associated with sufficient metadata to determine:

- Which model or method produced the result.
- Which version was used.
- When it was generated.
- Which relevant data period was used.

This supports reproducibility and debugging.

---

# 61. Reproducibility

Where practical, intelligence results should be reproducible from:

```text
Data Version
+
Model Version
+
Configuration
+
Parameters
```

Exact reproducibility requirements depend on the selected model technology.

---

# 62. Training vs Inference

The architecture should distinguish between:

### Training

Learning patterns from historical data.

### Inference

Using a trained model or method to generate a current prediction.

Conceptually:

```text
Historical Data
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Model
      ↓
Inference
      ↓
Prediction
```

These workloads may have different operational requirements.

---

# 63. Batch vs Real-Time Intelligence

Not every intelligence task needs real-time execution.

Potential classification:

| Capability                | Possible Execution Style   |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Dashboard metrics         | Near real-time / on demand |
| Demand forecasting        | Scheduled / on demand      |
| Anomaly detection         | Event-driven / scheduled   |
| Risk calculation          | Event-driven / scheduled   |
| Recommendations           | Event-driven / scheduled   |
| Scenario simulation       | On demand                  |
| Historical model training | Batch                      |

The final execution strategy must be based on actual requirements.

---

# 64. AI Failure Isolation

AI failures must be isolated from core operations.

Example:

```text
Forecasting Service
       ↓
Failure
       ↓
Forecast Unavailable
       ↓
Recommendation Generation Delayed
       ↓
Inventory Operations Continue
```

Core operational correctness must remain intact.

---

# 65. Fallback Strategy

Where an AI feature fails, StockPilot should use an appropriate fallback when practical.

Examples:

- Deterministic inventory rules.
- Previously validated forecast.
- Manual review.
- Basic threshold alerts.

The system must not fabricate intelligence results simply to avoid showing a failure.

---

# 66. No Hallucinated Operational Facts

If a language model is used anywhere in StockPilot, it must not invent:

- Inventory quantities.
- Supplier performance.
- Order states.
- Forecast values.
- Risk values.
- Transaction history.

Operational facts must come from authoritative system data.

---

# 67. AI Explanation Boundary

Explanations should be grounded in actual data.

Bad:

> "The AI believes demand will increase because the market seems strong."

Better:

> "Expected demand increased based on the recent demand trend and the selected forecasting model."

The explanation should not invent reasoning that was not actually used.

---

# 68. AI Observability

AI pipelines should expose enough information to diagnose failures.

Useful information includes:

- Processing status.
- Model version.
- Data period.
- Execution time.
- Failure reason.
- Evaluation metrics.
- Output status.

Sensitive model inputs should not be unnecessarily logged.

---

# 69. AI Cost Management

AI workloads should be designed with cost awareness.

Potential cost controls:

- Batch processing.
- Caching reusable results.
- Avoiding unnecessary model calls.
- Selecting appropriate models.
- Processing only changed data where possible.
- Monitoring inference/training workloads.

Cost optimization must not compromise correctness.

---

# 70. AI Latency

AI capabilities should have appropriate latency expectations.

Not every AI operation needs synchronous execution.

For example:

```text
User Request
     ↓
Forecast Generation
     ↓
Long Processing
     ↓
Asynchronous Result
```

may be preferable to blocking a normal user request.

---

# 71. AI and Realtime Events

Relevant operational events may trigger intelligence processing.

Example:

```text
Inventory Changed
       ↓
Risk Evaluation
       ↓
Stockout Risk Increased
       ↓
Recommendation Generated
       ↓
User Alert
```

The event architecture must ensure that duplicate events do not produce duplicate business effects.

---

# 72. AI Event Idempotency

If an event is processed more than once, the system should not create duplicate intelligence records unnecessarily.

Potentially affected outputs include:

- Forecasts.
- Anomalies.
- Risks.
- Recommendations.

The exact idempotency strategy must be defined in the implementation architecture.

---

# 73. AI Data Ownership

The intelligence layer owns generated outputs such as:

- Forecasts.
- Anomalies.
- Risk evaluations.
- Recommendations.

It does not own the underlying operational facts.

For example:

```text
Inventory = Operational Domain

Forecast using Inventory = Intelligence Domain
```

The forecast must not become the source of truth for inventory.

---

# 74. AI and Database Boundary

AI components should access data through controlled interfaces.

They should not receive unrestricted database write access.

Conceptually:

```text
Operational Data
      ↓
Controlled Data Interface
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Validated Intelligence Result
      ↓
Stored Intelligence State
```

---

# 75. AI and API Boundary

The API should expose useful intelligence capabilities without exposing unnecessary internal model details.

The frontend should interact with:

- Forecast results.
- Risk results.
- Anomaly results.
- Recommendations.
- Scenario results.

It should not need to understand internal model implementation.

# 80. AI Anti-Patterns

## 80.1 AI for the Sake of AI

Adding a chatbot or model without a real business problem provides little value.

---

## 80.2 Complex Model Before Baseline

A complex model without baseline comparison provides no evidence of improvement.

---

## 80.3 Training on Dirty Data

A model cannot repair fundamentally incorrect operational history.

---

## 80.4 Treating Predictions as Facts

Predictions must remain distinguishable from actual observations.

---

## 80.5 AI Bypassing Business Rules

AI must never directly override:

- Authorization.
- Inventory invariants.
- Order state rules.
- Transaction requirements.

---

## 80.6 Autonomous High-Impact Actions

Automatic execution without appropriate safeguards creates unnecessary operational risk.

---

## 80.7 Hallucinated Explanations

The system must not invent reasons for predictions or recommendations.

---

## 80.8 Data Leakage

Future information must not accidentally enter model training or evaluation.

---

## 80.9 Measuring Only Model Accuracy

The ultimate goal is operational improvement, not a better metric on a dataset.

---

# 81. AI Security Invariants

The following rules are mandatory.

## Invariant 1

AI output is never automatically trusted as operational truth.

## Invariant 2

AI cannot bypass authorization.

## Invariant 3

AI cannot directly bypass business rules.

## Invariant 4

AI cannot directly corrupt operational inventory state.

## Invariant 5

AI-generated recommendations must remain traceable.

## Invariant 6

Scenario simulations cannot modify production state.

## Invariant 7

Operational facts must come from authoritative system data.

## Invariant 8

Model failures must not corrupt core operations.

# 85. Final AI Principle

StockPilot should not aim to be an autonomous AI inventory system.

It should aim to be a **trustworthy intelligent decision system**.

The distinction matters.

The correct hierarchy is:

```text
Data
 ↓
Facts
 ↓
Analytics
 ↓
Predictions
 ↓
Risks
 ↓
Recommendations
 ↓
Human Decision
 ↓
Business Rules
 ↓
Operational Execution
```

AI provides intelligence.

The operational system provides truth.

Business rules provide control.

Human users provide judgment.

That separation is fundamental to StockPilot's architecture.
